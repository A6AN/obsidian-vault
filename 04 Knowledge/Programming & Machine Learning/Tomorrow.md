I still can't find the damn book.

It’s here somewhere — has to be. I promised her I’d read it. The Land of Stories — yeah, that one. The Disney fanfiction fever dream she swore by. I remember teasing her about it, like, “This sounds like something a thirteen-year-old writes in math class.” She didn’t care. She loved it.

I've been going through her stuff. Some to keep, some to donate. Mostly plushies. So many. Like, so many. Penguins, frogs, some non explainable creatures Why do we have this many?

A knock on the door pulls me out of it.

“Sir, we need to send these boxes,” the mover says — Bryan, I think.

“I know,” I mutter, not looking up. “Just... I need to find a book.”

He pauses. “Sir, we’re on a schedule. We’ve got other pickups—”

“I know, Bryan. Just...” I take a breath. “I really need this book. Like, really really need it.”

A beat.

“But sir, the—”

“Just f*** off, Bryan.”
The words leave my mouth before I can catch them. “Take the rest. Leave this box. Just go.”

He sighs, nods, and walks off. I don’t blame him. I wouldn’t want to deal with me either.

I should just ask her. She’d roll her eyes, curse me out, and then spend an hour tearing apart the room with me before realizing she lost it herself. She was no better at keeping track of her things than I am.

Ten boxes lie scattered on the floor like landmines. I open the next one.

Plushie.
Plushie.
Old hoodie.
A speaker — her favorite one, the one that played those cursed middle east-rock-jazz remixes at 2AM.
Another plushie. Oh wait—Sauce.

Not the food. The avocado plushie. Yeah, Sauce. She named an avocado Sauce. I remember arguing for days that it should be Guac, but nope — she stuck to Sauce. Said it “felt right.”

I smile, in spite of myself.

Then — a pair of chopsticks. Random.
Oh. From that Vietnamese place. First date. We went there so often the owner knew our names and orders. Told us we looked like siblings once. She laughed for ten minutes. I was mildly offended.

Next: a photo. Us. She’s laughing, and I’m trying to look cool while serving her pasta I made. It was great according to me. But she said it “tasted like effort.”

Another photo of us — blurry, on the subway. She liked bad photography. Said it was “real.”

Digging deeper. Please be here.

What’s this? A rose. Wilted. A chocolate wrapper. Faded gold foil, edges curled.

I pause.
Why is this here?

And then it hits me.

Valentine’s Day.

She didn’t have a date that year. Neither did I. I asked her out — got shut down instantly. She said she was spending the day with her other single friend. Fine. Cool. Whatever.

So I made a secret plan — she’d spend the day with her friend, and the evening with me. Romantic as hell. But I forgot one small detail: I never told her the plan.

She didn’t show.
I still gave her the rose and chocolate. My first gift like that.

A quiet laugh escapes me. A weak, almost broken thing.

Now I know. The book wasn’t in the boxes. It never was.

I walk to the bedroom.
Lift the mattress.
There it is — her favorite hiding spot. She used to call it her “vault of chaos.”

I pull the book out. It’s worn, pages folded, a post-it still stuck to chapter four: “Read this one first.”

I sit down on the bed. Hold the book in both hands. My fingers trace her handwriting.

I pick up my phone. Dial her number.

It rings once.
Then — voicemail.
