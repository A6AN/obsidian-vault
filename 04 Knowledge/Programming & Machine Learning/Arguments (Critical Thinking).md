# Arguments (Critical Thinking)

(The focus of this essay on the difference between Giving an argument and Having an argument)

The term **argument** is a very subjective word. The subject around the word "argument" explains the situation a person might be in—he might either be giving an argument or having an argument. If he is giving an argument, he must be proposing a greater question, a greater problem backed with facts, backed with evidence, and, most importantly, backed with an open mind to hear criticism, both for and against the argument he just set up. If he is having an argument, one can assume he is in an argument where he believes he is right, where he is driven more by emotion than by fact, where his mind is closed and won't believe otherwise. The two forms of argument are easily confused, even though they both serve very different purposes.

### 1. **Giving an Argument:**

When we give an argument in a critical way with logical thinking, we set forth an argument backed with facts, logic, and evidence to prove our point right. For example, think of a government project to improve the pollution situation in a certain locality. A person against the project would start by giving hard facts and evidence such as worse air quality, failure to reduce noise pollution from factories, or contamination in the lake. This kind of argument is backed by evidence and facts, which is aimed to persuade the listeners to the side of the speaker.

### 2. **Having an Argument:**

Contrary to giving an argument, having one is less about presenting facts and more about emotions and what the person personally feels, without taking a larger picture into account. It's about asserting one's own opinion onto others. For example, an argument between two friends about who is a better F1 driver—Michael Schumacher or Lewis Hamilton—instead of focusing on facts and figures, may stray from its path and be more about personal preferences and passion. Each person tries to 'win' by raising their volume and turning a blind eye to the hard facts provided. This type of argument is based more on emotion and passion.

Having overconfidence can lead a person from giving an argument to having one. Everyday exchanges between friends and people are generally thought of as a way to show the critical thinking prowess of an individual when, in fact, it is just an argument backed with emotions. Overconfidence acts as fuel to these flames; it adds to the emotional aspect of the argument and leads the argument away from logical reasoning.

Understanding the two different aspects of argument and having an open mind could be the right way to hone one's critical thinking skills and lead the person to be a better thinker.
